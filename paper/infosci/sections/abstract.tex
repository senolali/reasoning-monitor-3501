Large language models (LLMs) in multi-turn information systems accumulate
their own earlier replies and a growing task state in their context. We ask whether
this conversation history lowers reasoning accuracy, which is the cost in our
title, and whether process-level quality metrics warn before accuracy fails.
Each analysis was frozen before the outcomes it concerns were computed, and in
Study~1 before any data were collected. Study~1 contrasts, on 48 sharded math
tasks, identical user information with and without the model's prior replies.
Retaining the replies lowered final success by 0.21 for GPT-4o-mini and 0.29
for Mistral-7B (Holm-adjusted $p=0.026$ and $0.004$), and by 0.10 for Llama-4
Scout without significance. Study~2 introduces CDR-Stream, 100-turn
state-tracking dialogues with exact per-turn answers and model-specific
difficulty calibration. With full history, accuracy fell from about 0.6 in the
first five turns to about 0.1 after turn~20, even at matched difficulty, while
a solver-verified state summary held it 0.52--0.59 higher (Holm-adjusted
$p<0.001$). Consistency, logical coherence and stability often stayed level or
rose as accuracy collapsed, remaining reassuring while answers became wrong. No such metric, paired readiness measure
or calibrated single-stream drift detector gave robust, model-general early
warning; in
Study~2, the metrics that need repeated samples were complete only for
GPT-4o-mini. A detectability bound and a simulation show that one 100-turn
stream reveals only very large drops, whereas pooling sessions makes realistic
drops detectable. Systems should therefore keep task state outside the transcript and
monitor across sessions.
